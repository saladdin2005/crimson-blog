## The real problem isn't a shortage of tools

After two years of non-stop releases, the problem is the exact opposite: we don't lack AI tools — we drown in tools that **promise everything and deliver little**. Open your own list right now and count the apps you tried once and forgot.

Before paying for another subscription, start from the right question: *which task actually eats my time?* A good tool doesn't get added to the list — it **replaces** an old, expensive habit.

## Five criteria worth using

1. **It saves measurable time** — count minutes, not vibes. If it saves you 30 minutes a day summarizing meetings, it's worth it. If it "inspires" you, it's not a tool.
2. **It fits your current workflow** — anything that forces a new standalone app will be abandoned in two weeks. Look for browser extensions and integrations you'll actually use.
3. **You control your data** — read the data policy before uploading anything private. If you don't know where your data goes, you're selling it cheap.
4. **Pricing has no surprises** — free tiers are generous, but usage-based pricing can shock you at month's end.
5. **A simple fallback exists** — if the tool vanished tomorrow (quite possible), could you do the same task by hand? If not, you've tied your work to something fragile.

## What actually changed this year?

The biggest shift isn't raw model power — it's the **micro-tools** built on top: an assistant inside your editor, automatic inbox triage, code review as you type. Real value came from bringing AI closer to the task, not from waiting on an empty prompt box.

> Practical rule: try one tool for two weeks, measure saved time in numbers, then decide. Chasing every release is the most expensive way to waste the time you hoped to save.

## Bottom line

Don't collect tools. Build a **small set** of three or four you master and use daily. Depth in one tool always beats window-shopping twenty.
