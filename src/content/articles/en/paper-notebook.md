The book tells you: "The rich make money work for them." It sounds great, and we all love to hear it.

But if we look a little closer, the path it draws goes through almost one single door: **the bank giving you money.** You take a loan, buy something that earns, and that thing pays back the loan from its profits.

And here is a simple question: what if the bank doesn't give it to you?

Let's test it. Instead of debating the book in theory, we'll hand its advice to an ordinary young man and see what happens to him.

## Meet Karim

Karim is 24. He works as an employee on an average salary. He pays rent for his room, helps his family now and then, and what is left at the end of the month is small. All his savings would last a few months if he lost his job.

He owns no house and no land. He has no rich relative to vouch for him. And he has never taken a loan in his life, so the banks have no "history" on him.

*(Karim is a fictional person. But you probably know more than one person like him.)*

During a short vacation he read *Rich Dad Poor Dad* and loved it. He felt that someone was finally talking about money clearly. And he told himself: "I'll start today, and I'll follow the book to the letter."

## Step One: "Buy Assets"

The most important rule in the book is easy: **an asset puts money in your pocket, and a liability takes money out of your pocket.** The apartment you live in is a liability. The apartment you rent out is an asset.

Karim asked himself: "What asset can I afford to buy?"

He opened the listing sites. A small apartment to rent out? Its price is many times everything he has saved. A small shop? Same thing. Even if he bought stocks with everything he had, the profits would be tiny.

Then he remembered what the book says in this situation: **use other people's money.** In other words: take a loan. In the book's eyes, the rich don't pay from their own pockets. They borrow, buy the asset, and let it pay off the loan by itself.

So Karim said: "Then I'll start with the bank."

## Step Two: The Bank's Door

Karim walked into the bank smiling and explained his idea with excitement. The employee listened politely, then began asking:

- Do you have a steady income?
- Do you have property or something else we can hold as collateral for the loan?
- Do you have a guarantor?
- Have you taken a loan before and paid it back?
- Do you have a study and numbers for the project?

The only question he could answer "yes" to was the first one: his salary from his job. The same job the book looks down on!

And this is not Karim's problem alone. A survey by the World Bank and the Union of Arab Banks found that **only one in five** small and medium-sized companies in our region has a loan or a bank credit line. The World Bank economist **Roberto Rocha** said that the share of these companies' loans in total bank lending in the region is about 7.6%. And experts at the International Finance Corporation (IFC) talk about a shortage of collateral and a weak financial system.

These numbers are not new, and later reports say progress has been slow.

Note also that these numbers cover companies that **already exist**. A young man starting from zero is even further from the bank than they are.

The economist **Hernando de Soto** explained part of the problem in his book *The Mystery of Capital*. He said that poor people may own many things, but they are not legally registered, so the bank doesn't accept them as collateral. Meaning: what you own may be worth nothing in the bank's eyes.

## Step Three: And After the Rejection?

Karim doesn't give up easily. He has three roads. And each road has a paradox.

**Road one: a personal loan, or money from family.** He'll take the money and start. But who will pay the installment every month? His salary. So money will leave his pocket every month, which is exactly what the book calls a "liability," and what it warns against.

**Road two: courses for the wealthy.** He says: "I'll learn first before I start." But these courses cost money. An article in **Slate** magazine mentioned that seminars linked to the book's brand drew many complaints from participants. And Karim would pay from his already small savings.

**Road three: jump into a business with heavy debt.** This is the road the book loves. It has everything except safety.

And here is an important lesson from **Nassim Taleb** in his book *Fooled by Randomness*. He separates two kinds of loss: a loss you can recover from, and a loss that knocks you out of the game completely. A person with big money can take a fall and get back up. But Karim, without enough savings and without a safety net, could be chasing one fall in debt for years.

And even if the loan arrives, success is not guaranteed. Economic researchers, including **Abhijit Banerjee, Dean Karlan and Jonathan Zinman**, ran structured experiments on small loans given to poor people to start businesses. They found that the effect was **modest** on average, not a big change in families' lives. A loan is not a magic wand.

## What Happens After a Year?

Let's imagine. Karim took a personal loan and opened a small online store without enough experience. Sales are weak. The monthly installment eats half his salary. He starts to fall behind on payments, sleeps little, and tells himself the book tricked him.

This is not certain. Some people really do succeed this way. But it is very possible, and we rarely hear about it, because the one who fails doesn't write a book.

Now let's go back to the beginning and look at another road:

Suppose Karim decided to start **with no debt**. He stayed in his job because it pays his bills. After work he started offering a small service he's good at to a few clients. With his profit he bought better tools, then more clients.

Slow? Yes. Boring? Maybe. But if he makes a mistake, he loses only a little, learns a lot, and doesn't end up in debt.

To be clear: this is my own suggestion to illustrate the idea, not from the book. And the book would most likely consider it too small.

## What the Book Didn't Say

The book tells us that its author grew up next to the son of the "rich dad," in an environment of business, connections, and opportunities. And this is actually a very important element in the story: a **network of connections** that opens doors.

Karim doesn't have this network. And he doesn't have collateral. And the book never stops at this gap, as if everyone starts from the same line.

But reality isn't like that. The economist **Thomas Piketty** argues in his book *Capital in the Twenty-First Century* that those who own capital often build their wealth faster than wages grow over long periods. Meaning the starting point matters a great deal, and the one who starts with money or connections is ahead of the one who starts with nothing.

## The Comparison at a Glance

| What the book suggests | Reality for a young man with no collateral | Suggested practical alternatives |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Borrow to buy an asset | The bank asks for collateral, steady income, and a record with banks | Start with no debt: a small service or product at the lowest cost |
| Buy property that earns income | Its price is many times his savings | Build a small asset: a skill, clients, content, a simple shop |
| Don't work for money alone | His job is his only source of funding | Keep the job as a source of income while you start |
| Learn from the rich | Some paid education is expensive | Start with free sources, and with people who came before you in your field |

*These columns illustrate the idea and are not a study. And the best alternative differs from one country to another.*

## So What Remains Useful in the Book?

We don't want to be unfair to the book. It has useful things for Karim that don't require a bank:

- Understanding the difference between income and expense, and between an asset and a liability.
- Stopping the habit of buying what he consumes without counting.
- Learning early, and talking about money without shame.

And research supports this. **Annamaria Lusardi** and **Olivia Mitchell** (2014) found that people who understand money better tend to make better decisions and build more wealth.

But there is a difference between two things: **understanding money**, and **getting the money to start**. And the book sometimes talks as if the first replaces the second.

## The Bottom Line

Karim's experiment says the book's philosophy is not a lie, but it is **written for someone who can knock on the bank's door and have it opened.** As for the one who doesn't yet have the key, he has to start from the other side: with a small step, little debt, and long patience.

And the most important question is not "How do I get rich?" but:

**"What can I start with today, with what I have?"**
