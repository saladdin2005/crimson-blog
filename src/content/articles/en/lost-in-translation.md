Imagine waking up tomorrow to find your inner voice has vanished.

I don't mean your speaking voice. I mean that small voice in your head — the one that comments on everything, jokes with you, and comforts you as you fall asleep. You open your mouth and correct words come out, but they are cold, as though rented.

That is exactly what happened to a girl named Eva Hoffman, when she was thirteen.

## Thirteen Years Old… and One Suitcase

Eva was born in 1945 in the Polish city of Kraków. She grew up among books and music and family conversations, and she knew everything about her world: its jokes, the smell of its streets, and the way the word “friend” was said.

  <img src="/images/krakow-1950s.webp" alt="A street in Poland back then" loading="lazy" width="720" height="462" />

Then in 1959 her family packed their things and emigrated to Canada.

At her new school, the first thing that happened was that the teacher changed her name. She became “Eva” instead of the Polish “Ewa,” and her sister Alina became “Ellen.” It was a small change on paper, but it was the first sign: here, they will write you all over again.

## Three Stages of One Journey

Eva divided her book into three parts, as if to say that the journey has clear chapters.

First: Paradise. Here she returns to her childhood in Poland. She writes about a world she understood from the inside, a world where every word was in its place and every joke made her laugh without anyone having to explain a thing.

Second: Exile. Here comes the shock. A new country, a new school, and a language she can catch only fragments of. She feels she is standing behind glass: she sees everyone talking and laughing, and hears nothing.

Third: The New World. Here the more beautiful story begins. She does not go back, and she does not forget what she was; she builds a new place for herself between the two languages.

And that is what makes the book close to you even if you never emigrated. We have all passed through this “glass” stage: the first day at a new job, the first class in a language we don't know, or the first time we tried to write a sentence in a second language and felt ashamed of our mistakes.

## When Words Turn into Empty Sounds

Years later Eva wrote her famous book “Lost in Translation: A Life in a New Language” (published in 1989), in which she recounts her experience with rare honesty.

Its strongest idea is simple and painful: a word is not just a translation. In your mother tongue, a word is loaded with memories, smells and feelings; in the new language it may be perfectly correct and perfectly empty.

Eva describes knowing that a word like “river” exists in English, yet it stirred nothing in her. In the Polish of her childhood the word meant a whole feeling; in English it was just a sound.

And that made her feel she was living with only half a personality. She understood everything and felt nothing.

## Does This Really Happen? Science Says Yes

You might assume this is just a writer's sensitivity. But researchers have studied the phenomenon and reached similar findings.

Aneta Pavlenko, a professor of psycholinguistics, wrote about the relationship between languages and emotions in multilingual people. Among her most striking findings is that many feel their first language is closer to the heart, while a second language can give them a distance that makes talking about difficult emotions easier.

Lera Boroditsky, a cognitive scientist, studies how language shapes the way we think. Her work shows that your language can change what you pay attention to in the world, making you notice some details and overlook others.

Long before them, the philosopher Ludwig Wittgenstein made his famous remark in *Tractatus Logico-Philosophicus*: “The limits of my language mean the limits of my world.”

And Goethe said before that: “He who does not know a foreign language knows nothing of his own.”

On that basis, when you learn a new language you are not merely adding words to your dictionary. You are opening new windows onto yourself and onto the world.

## The Turning Point: When the New Language Began to Feel Like Hers

If the book stopped here it would be a sad book. The beautiful thing is that Eva did not remain stuck between two languages.

Over the years, through reading and writing and living inside English, the new words slowly filled with meaning. She began to dream in English. And the inner voice she had lost began to return — this time with a new accent.

She studied in America and became a writer and critic working for the most important cultural publications, and she wrote the book itself in English. The language that had nearly swallowed her became the tool with which she told her story to the world.

And the gain was not compensation for the loss. It was something third: a person carrying two worlds and seeing with two eyes.

## How Eva Writes: A Style That Examines Words Under a Microscope

You might expect a book about exile and longing to be full of tears and grand phrases. Eva does the exact opposite.

First: she is calm. She does not raise her voice or ask you to feel sorry. She tells the scene as it is and leaves you to feel it yourself. It is precisely this calmness that makes the wound deeper.

Second: she starts with something small and ends with a big idea. One word, a name changed at school, a sentence she didn't understand — then suddenly the picture widens into a question about identity, memory and belonging. As if she were holding a grain of sand and showing you the whole beach through it.

Third: she writes and thinks at the same time. She is not only a storyteller but a smart reader of her own experience. She works in the world of books and criticism, and it shows in her method: she stops at a word and asks where it came from, what it carries, and what it lost along the way.

Fourth: the lovely paradox. This writer who recounts how her first language slipped away from her tells her story in English — the language that came to her late. In doing so she follows the path of other writers who changed their literary language, like the Polish-born Joseph Conrad, who wrote his novels in English too.

This is why you don't read the book as an ordinary memoir. You read it as if listening to a clever friend recount the hardest period of her life, without asking for your pity, and leaving you at the end thinking about your own language.

## Why This Book Matters to You

Because Eva's story tells you things grammar books don't:

First: learning a language is not just a school project; it is an inner journey. Sometimes you will feel stupid, sometimes lost — and that is completely normal.

Second: silence at the beginning is not failure. It is a stage in which the ground is being built under your feet.

Third: every language you know adds a new person inside you. You will not be quite the same when you speak English or French, and that is not a flaw — it is the prize.

## And You, Crimson Reader… Where Do You Stand in This Story?

You might say: I never emigrated, so what does Eva have to do with me?

But think about it. Wherever you are, you already live among many languages: the language of home, the language of school, the language of offices and streets, the language of screens and work, and sometimes the language of family and roots. Each one carries a different version of you.

Have you noticed how you joke in one language and apologize in another? How you love in one language and get angry in another? That is exactly what Eva was trying to understand. The difference is that she discovered it through pain, and you can discover it through curiosity.

And so learning a new language is not just a way to get a job or a certificate. It is a way to try out another version of yourself — perhaps bolder, perhaps lighter, perhaps truer than the one you are used to.

## The Bottom Line

“Lost in Translation” is not only a book about emigration, nor only about languages. It is a book about a question we all ask in one way or another: who am I when I speak with a tongue that is not my own?

And Eva's answer, after many years: you do not lose yourself. You expand.

So if you are learning a new language now, and your words feel strange in your mouth, remember a thirteen-year-old girl who once stood in a Canadian classroom understanding only half of what was said. Years later she wrote a book the world reads.
