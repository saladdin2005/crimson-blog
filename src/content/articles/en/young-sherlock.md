## The Question No One Dared to Ask

For a hundred and eighty years we have known Sherlock Holmes as he is today: a cold, organized man who walks into the room and tells you where you were yesterday from the mud stain on your shoe.

But have you ever thought about the frightening question? Where did this man come from? What happened to him to make him this cold? Who hurt him? And who failed him? And who taught him to hide his heart behind his mind?

Conan Doyle answered this question somewhat grudgingly when he let the detective recount a few scraps of his youth to Watson. In "The Gloria Scott", Holmes tells of his first case at university, when his friend Victor Trevor's dog bit him — the bite that set in motion a friendship that led him into crime. And in "The Musgrave Ritual" he mentions that he lived in a small flat near the British Museum while he was still finding his way.

That is all. Doyle never wrote a complete story about young Holmes. He left us a wide empty space, and there is no empty space that does not tempt someone to fill it.

And in March 2026, someone came to fill it.

Who was the madman who did it?

On the fourth of March 2026, Prime Video released Young Sherlock — all eight episodes at once — in more than two hundred and forty countries and territories. Behind it is a name that action fans know well: Guy Ritchie, who directed the two Sherlock Holmes films with Robert Downey Jr. a few years ago.

But this series is completely different. It has nothing to do with the old films, and it is drawn from a series of novels by Andrew Lane about Holmes in his adolescence. The lead role goes to a young British actor named Hero Fiennes Tiffin, whom Harry Potter audiences already know.

![Hero Fiennes Tiffin as Sherlock Holmes at his desk in a scene from Young Sherlock](/images/young-sherlock-holmes.webp)

As for the story? I don't want to spoil anything for you here. All I will say is that you will find a rebellious young man who has just come out of prison, an older brother — Mycroft — who pays his bail and then sends him to Oxford University to keep him out of trouble. And there a crime will turn everything upside down, professors will fall one after another, and a thread of secrets will stretch from the halls of the university to a place much closer than you imagine: the family home itself.

So let us stop at this point, because it is the very heart of the whole series.

## What Did Doyle Actually Say About His Youth?

Before we enter Oxford, let us go back to the source. What did Doyle leave us about young Holmes?

The answer: a girl.

In "The Greek Interpreter", Holmes tells his friend Watson that there is something of the artist in his blood, and that his grandmother was the sister of the French painter Vernezhe. And in the same story we hear for the first time of a brother named Mycroft, and Holmes says of him that he has a greater power of observation than I do, but lacks ambition and energy. And in "The Gloria Scott" we read about the first case of his life at university, and about the one single friend who bound him in friendship there.

One friend. Only.

Notice this small detail. Doyle himself drew Holmes as a young man with few friends, living in a small room, reading, meditating, and investigating. Then the series comes along to ask: what if this only friend was more dangerous than we imagined?

This is how good stories are made. They do not contradict the original; they enter through the cracks it left.

## When Holmes Stepped Off the Page: The Story of George Edalji

You might think all this is mere fiction. But in 1903, in a small English village, a story happened that resembles the plots we love — except that it was real, and its hero was made of flesh and blood.

His name was George Edalji. A shy young solicitor from Staffordshire, the son of a priest of Indian origin in an English village, and one of the top pupils at his school. And during that period strange incidents began to shake the region: animals killed and mutilated in the dark of night.

And the easiest thing to do was to point at the strange young man in the village. And that is exactly what happened. Edalji was accused and sentenced to hard labour, and sources say that his colour and origin played a part in directing suspicion at him.

He came out of prison in 1906 after serving part of his sentence, but the accusation kept trailing him like a heavy shadow. He was not truly free, for no one believed him.

Then his case reached a man no one would have thought would care: Arthur Conan Doyle himself. Doyle met him — a doctor before he was a writer — and did exactly what Holmes would have done. He asked him nothing. He watched how he read. He saw a man holding the paper close to his face to make out the letters, and understood in an instant that his eyes were very weak. A weakness that made it impossible for him to roam dark fields at night, attack animals, and evade the police night after night.

Doyle consulted eye doctors about a letter published in two medical journals. Then in January 1907 he published a two-part investigation in the Daily Telegraph that shook public opinion. The road was not easy: the chief of police did not welcome his intervention, and sources say Doyle himself received threatening letters. But in August 1907 Edalji was cleared of the charge of mutilation.

And there is a small detail that makes the story prettier: it is said that Edalji attended Doyle's wedding in the same year.

He took this lesson he had learned from his teacher Joseph Bell — that close attention can save a man — and turned it from a story into justice. And the story later inspired the novelist Julian Barnes to write his novel Arthur and George.

Now ask yourself: how many "George Edaljis" have passed through your life? Someone judged by everyone for his appearance or his name or his origin, while no one noticed the small details that would have changed everything? Or perhaps you were that person one day?

Here precisely lies Holmes's strength. Not in his intelligence, but in his refusal to judge before he looks.

## When the Mystery Becomes Personal

In Doyle's original stories, Holmes is not pained by the case. The case is a beautiful puzzle on his table; he smiles at it, then solves it and moves on.

But here the mystery comes through his own door.

And this is the essential difference between the seasoned Holmes and the young Holmes. The first learned to control his feelings until they turned into a mask. The second has not learned yet. He is biased, he is emotional, he runs after the truth instead of walking towards it.

And this is exactly what psychology says about adolescence. Professor Laurence Steinberg, a psychologist at Temple University, argues in his book Age of Opportunity that adolescence is a period when the mind is more malleable than at any other time — that is, it is both an opportunity and a danger. And before him the psychologist Erik Erikson said that the adolescent fights a bitter struggle to know who he is, or else he is lost in "diffusion".

Doesn't this picture look like a very smart, exhausted, lost young man?

This is the Holmes whose life you enter and find he resembles no one you know. He cannot control his nerves, he does not wait calmly for evidence, he grows angry when those he loves fail him, and he feels the whole world is a family of liars.

And as for liars, there is another person who stole the show from Holmes himself.

## The Friend Who Will Become an Enemy

In "The Final Problem", when Holmes faces Professor Moriarty, he describes him as a mathematician who at the age of twenty-one wrote a paper on the theory of the double tangent. That is approximately all we know of his past. No childhood, no friends, no heart.

But this series turns the table: Moriarty here is Holmes's close friend, played by the actor Dónal Finn.

Imagine that for a moment. Two men who will one day stand at the edge of a waterfall in Switzerland, each trying to kill the other, began their lives side by side as companions — laughing, quarrelling, and solving puzzles together.

![Sherlock Holmes and Moriarty in an action scene from Young Sherlock](/images/young-sherlock-moriarty.webp)

And this is the strongest part of the series. And I think the reason is simple: we know the ending, and they do not.

Every time one of them reaches out a hand to the other, your heart skips a beat. And every time they exchange an honest look, you wonder: when, do you think, will the break come? And where will the first crack be?

This is the old game of tragedy — that the audience knows what the characters do not. When we know the two friends will part, every moment of friendship between them becomes painfully precious.

## The Greatest Absence: Watson

There is a question that slips into your mind as you watch, and perhaps you will not notice it until the last episode: where is Dr. Watson?

In Doyle's stories the two meet for the first time in A Study in Scarlet, when Holmes effectively begins his career. Meaning that everything in this series happens before he meets the friend who will accompany him for the rest of his life.

And this changes the meaning of the story entirely. There is no Watson here to narrate about him in amazement, nor to soften his edge, nor to tell him he is exaggerating. Here is a young man who needs a friend, and he finds one in Moriarty.

And when you think about it, you realize the hidden tragedy: perhaps Holmes was looking for Watson all his life, and perhaps Watson was the second chance that fate brought after the first friend who wounded him. As if the original stories, which we know by heart, carry inside them an old wound we never saw.

This is a personal reading of mine, nothing more. But it is what made me look again at every scene that brought Holmes and Watson together after that.

How beautiful it is when a new story rearranges your feelings toward an old story you love!

## Who Stands Behind the Camera?

This was no small project. It was filmed in Britain, Spain, and Wales between the summer of 2024 and early 2025; Guy Ritchie directed its first two episodes and served as executive producer, while Matthew Parkhill wrote and ran it.

The cast features heavyweight names: Joseph Fiennes and Natasha McElhone as Sherlock's parents, Max Irons as his brother Mycroft, and Colin Firth as a striking guest, alongside Zin Tseng as a mysterious character at the heart of the events.

![The cast of Young Sherlock in a group photo](/images/young-sherlock-cast.webp)

And note that Ritchie, known for the fast pace and wild editing of his previous Holmes films, delivered here a work that differs in form. One critic pointed out that the series hardly resembles his detective films visually. As if the man realized that young Sherlock does not need showmanship, but a heart.

## What the Critics Said

Not everyone agreed on the series, and that is natural.

TechRadar praised the series as more than just a detective solving crimes, and found it a work full of vitality. Meanwhile some critics — including a review in Malaysia's The Star that gave it 6.5 out of 10 — felt the series needed more investigation and more quiet. Other reviews noted that the last two episodes felt rushed and that some characters did not get the development they deserved.

But there was near consensus on one point: the Holmes–Moriarty duo is the pillar that carries the whole work.

And this brings us back to an important question: are we drawn to the story because it "solves a puzzle"? Or because it gives us what we lack in a world growing ever lonelier?

I lean toward the second.

## Why Do We Love Watching the Great Ones Stumble?

There is a simple psychological reason behind this wave of "origin" stories: we want to believe that greatness can be made.

When we see the complete Holmes, we applaud him and go back to our lives. But when we see him as a young man stumbling, making mistakes, getting angry, and losing, we draw closer to him. We tell ourselves: perhaps this confused young man I see in the mirror is also on his way to something.

![Young Sherlock Holmes in a bowler hat standing in front of an omnibus](/images/young-sherlock-street.webp)

These are hope stories disguised as crime.

And this is what the series, adapted from Andrew Lane's novels, was built on. The question is not how he became great. The question is what price he paid to become so. And the price each time is human: losing a friend, a family's betrayal, or a mother collapsing before his eyes.

## The Brother Who Pays the Price in Silence

One of the most beautiful things in the series is that it gives Mycroft real space. In the original stories this brother appears rarely, like a ghost who rules Britain from behind a desk. But here we see him intervene, pay the bail, and set things in order, knowing that his younger brother is a bomb walking on two legs.

And in this choice there is a hidden tenderness. For this cold brother we know from the books was originally a guardian, not a jailer. He protects his brother from himself as much as he protects him from the world.

And anyone with an older brother knows this feeling exactly: that gratitude mixed with resentment.

## Three Reasons to Watch It Tonight

First: the friendship. You will find in the Holmes–Moriarty duo a rare chemistry that makes you laugh with them in one scene and ache in the next.

Second: the family mystery. This is not a crime series you forget after a single episode. There is a long thread running from the first episode to the last, and whoever catches it enjoys it all the more.

Third: the pace. Only eight episodes, all released at once. No stalling, no filler, and no waiting a whole week to know what happened.

If you are a fan of the old Holmes, you will find roots you recognize. And if you have not read a word of Doyle, you will not be lost — you may even leave wanting to open his first book.

What shocked viewers most in the ending is that the series's real enemy was not in Oxford. He was in the house. The final episodes reveal that Silas Holmes, Sherlock's father — played by Joseph Fiennes — is the mastermind behind the conspiracy, and that the secrets the family buried were far darker than any murder. And his mother Cordelia becomes painfully entangled in this story; we see her in an asylum, and her scattered words turn into evidence.

And right here the story turns from a police puzzle into a family tragedy.

Some critics say Silas represents "the dark version" of what Sherlock could become: a brilliant mind that sees the next steps before anyone else, but is walking in the wrong direction. And this is an idea that echoes in everything Doyle wrote. Genius is a double-edged sword, and whoever owns it must decide which side to stand on.

And before the series closes its door, it leaves us with something more dangerous. Moriarty, Sherlock's friend, is drawn to the equations that lay behind all this chaos. And in his eyes there is something that was not there in the first episode.

Here you can hear the sound of history moving. That small crack that began to widen between two Oxford friends is the very same one that will one day lead them both to the abyss.

The series has been renewed for a second season, as Prime Video announced, along with new actors joining it.

## What Remains After the Last Episode?

Let us return to the question we began with: where did this man come from?

The answer the series offers is not final, nor should it be. It is one of thousands of possible answers. But it tells us something deeper than any plot.

The cold genius we love was not born cold. He learned coldness because he needed it. He learned to hide his heart because his heart was broken more than once.

And this is perhaps the most beautiful point a story can make about Holmes. That the closed doors we see in adults were once open, before someone walked through them and ruined everything.

And here is a question worth pausing on: is the loneliness we see in Holmes a choice or a wound?

I think the honest answer is both. We start withdrawing because we were hurt, then we convince ourselves that we chose it freely, and then with time we forget the difference between the two. And this is what makes a character like Holmes closer to us than we admit. How many of us say "I like quiet" when we are really protecting ourselves from an old disappointment?

A good story does not give you the answer; it puts the mirror in front of your face.

And so when we return to Doyle's stories and read of Holmes in his room on Baker Street playing his violin alone at night, silence takes on a different weight. He is no longer merely an eccentric man. He is a man who carries a past.

And now, a final question I leave for you:

> If your closest friend were to become your most dangerous enemy years from now, would you love him just as honestly today?
